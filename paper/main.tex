\title{Reconstructing Wound Surfaces and Planning Conservative Filling: An Evidence-Bounded Computational Study}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Diana Rold\'an}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Tecnol\'ogico de Monterrey\\Monterrey, Mexico}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Before planning how to fill a wound, a computer must estimate the visible
surface and decide where filling should stop. These are different problems:
the surface of the wound can be observed, but the original, uninjured skin
above it is generally unknown. We evaluate whether the available observations
justify estimating that missing surface directly, or whether the system should
plan partial filling in stages and require a final target supplied by an
operator. Our evidence combines computer-generated wound data, published
reconstructions of a silicone wound model, and offline software simulations.
The depth maps in the sensor experiments are rendered from synthetic meshes,
not captured by a physical depth camera. The clean-measurement test accepted
0 of 24 variants. In a separate test that hid healthy skin, an uncertainty
band covered 88.32\% of evaluated points, below its 90\% target. A comparison
of 18 synthetic carved wounds failed the predefined criteria for relying on a
directly inferred target. We therefore select staged partial filling with an
operator-defined final target as a computational design, not as a validated
treatment. The calibration checks also failed their overall engineering gate.
No patient study, physical printing, physical robot execution, or independent
metric-scale recovery is demonstrated.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Wound geometry, shape evaluation, uncertainty, sensor routing, conservative
planning, computational validation
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction: What Are We Trying to Measure?}

Imagine looking at a shallow bowl. A picture may show its outline and color,
but a plan to fill it also needs its three-dimensional (3D) shape and a
decision about how high the filling should go. A wound presents a similar,
but more difficult, problem. A computer may estimate the currently visible
wound bed---the surface at the bottom of the wound---and some surrounding
skin. It cannot simply look at the wound and observe the skin that was there
before the injury. That missing surface matters because a different assumed
top surface can produce a different estimate of the space to be filled.
In this paper, a \emph{surface-conformal} plan means a proposed pattern that
follows the shape of the observed bed rather than assuming it is flat.

We study two computational choices. The first is to infer a final surface
from the healthy-looking skin around the wound and plan directly to that
surface. The second is to plan small, conservative partial fills, update the
observed geometry between stages, and require an operator to specify the
final target. A \emph{target} is a surface the plan aims toward; it is not
automatically the patient's original skin or a clinical prescription.

The research question is: \emph{Do the available observations justify direct
filling to an inferred surface, or should the computational design require
staged partial filling and an externally specified final target?} We answer
with previously saved computational results. This is not a clinical trial,
and the framework does not issue instructions to a physical printer or robot.

\section{Concepts Needed to Read the Results}

\subsection{Images, Depth, and 3D Surfaces}

An RGB image records red, green, and blue color values at each image point, or
pixel. A depth map records an estimated distance from a camera to the visible
surface at each supported pixel. RGB-D combines color and depth information.
Here, the depth maps in the sensor comparisons are generated by rendering a
known synthetic 3D surface, called a \emph{mesh}; they are not readings from
a real depth camera. A mesh represents a surface using points connected into
small triangles. A point cloud is a collection of 3D points without
necessarily connecting them into triangles.

A \emph{mask} marks the image pixels assigned to the wound region. The masks
used in the relevant synthetic comparisons were reviewed and fixed before
evaluation, but they are not expert clinical labels. A \emph{route} is one
way of providing images and depth to the reconstruction. For example, an
RGB-only route uses color images, while an RGB-D route also uses depth. Low
coverage means depth is missing in some locations; noise means the depth
values are disturbed; and misalignment means the color and depth do not
refer to the same locations accurately.

\subsection{The Missing Reference Surface}

To discuss a depth or a filling volume, one must choose a surface from which
to measure. We call this the \emph{reference surface}. In the bowl analogy,
changing the height of the imagined waterline changes the computed volume,
even when the bottom of the bowl stays the same. Likewise, a computed
reference volume in this paper depends on both the observed wound-bed
estimate and the chosen reference. It is not a measurement of the true
amount of missing tissue.

An \emph{uncertainty envelope} is a lower and upper range of predicted
heights. It communicates how uncertain a model is about a surface, not a
guarantee that the correct height lies inside the range. \emph{Coverage}
is the fraction of evaluated points where a known test height lies inside
that range. For example, 90\% coverage would mean that 90 out of every 100
evaluated points, on average, were inside the envelope in that test; it
would not mean the system is safe for 90\% of patients.

\subsection{What the Error Numbers Mean}

To compare two surfaces, we measure how far sampled points on an estimated
surface are from a reference surface. The mean absolute error (MAE) is the
average of those nonnegative distances. The 95th percentile (P95) is a
distance at or below which 95\% of the measured errors fall. A lower value
is better for these two error measures.

When distances are expressed as a percentage of wound width, a value of 1\%
means one hundredth of that width; it does not by itself give a distance in
millimeters. \emph{Completeness} asks the opposite question: what fraction
of reference points have an estimated surface within a stated distance?
Higher completeness is better, but only for the available reference region
and the chosen tolerance. Reference-volume error is the absolute difference
between two computed volumes divided by the declared synthetic reference
volume. It is not an error against a known clinical tissue volume.

A \emph{quality gate} is a predefined check that returns a pass, rejection,
or need for more information. A false alarm occurs when a clean configuration
is flagged; a missed fault occurs when a faulty configuration is not flagged.
These are research checks, not certified safety tests. Some checks use only
observations that could be available to the system; other checks use a known
reference for evaluation and cannot be used as if it were available in
ordinary operation.

\section{Methods: Data, Algorithms, and Tests}

\subsection{Where the Evidence Comes From}

Figure~\ref{fig:evidence} and Table~\ref{tab:datasets} separate four sources:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Syn3DWound} is a public, computer-generated dataset with
    RGB images, meshes, and camera information
    (\href{https://doi.org/10.25919/5rwz-ts17}{DOI: 10.25919/5rwz-ts17}).
    We use selected sequences for shape comparisons and synthetic depth
    experiments. The depth is rendered from the source mesh, so agreement
    with that mesh is not an independent test of a physical sensor.
    \item \textbf{SALVE} contains images and scanner data from silicone wound
    models, not patients
    (\href{https://doi.org/10.25919/cvhk-pc32}{DOI: 10.25919/cvhk-pc32}).
    We evaluate selected reconstructions published by the dataset authors;
    we do not claim to have trained those reconstruction methods here.
    \item \textbf{Held-out synthetic tests} evaluate calibration checks and
    attempts to infer hidden healthy surfaces. A held-out test is a portion
    reserved for evaluation rather than selecting the method.
    \item \textbf{Procedural software cases} are generated scenes used to
    check how the offline planning interface responds to specific conditions.
    They are not camera recordings or physical robot trials.
\end{itemize}

For the reported SALVE comparisons, the reconstruction is aligned to the
scanner reference by adjusting position, orientation, and scale. This is
called ground-truth (GT) similarity alignment. It allows a comparison of
shape after using the reference to set scale; it does \emph{not} show that
the system can recover real-world scale independently. The selected
Syn3DWound RGB shape comparison also uses evaluation-only similarity
alignment. Synthetic dataset units are not certified millimeters.

\subsection{How to Read This Methods Section}

This section is written so that a reader can repeat every step. For each
step we give: what goes in, what comes out, the rule or formula, the
numbers we used, and why we chose them. All code lives in the repository.
The reconstruction, routing, and calibration experiments are in the
notebook \texttt{notebooks/04\_rgb\_wound\_3d\_reconstruction.ipynb}. The
offline planner is in \texttt{robot/simulation/observable\_demo.py}. The
unified pipeline is in \texttt{pipeline/unified.py}, and the patient-level
pipeline is in \texttt{pipeline/patient.py}, with the coverage planner in
\texttt{pipeline/coverage.py}. A few early experiment scripts are not in the
repository. For those steps we give the exact values and name the sealed
result file that records them, instead of guessing.

\emph{Units.} Syn3DWound geometry comes in the dataset's own length unit,
which we call a dataset unit (DU). It has not been certified as a meter or a
millimeter, so we never convert it. Small lengths are written in milli-DU
(mDU; 1~mDU $=10^{-3}$~DU). Many errors are given as a percentage of the
wound's width, so that wounds of different sizes can be compared.

\subsection{Step 1: Turning Images into a Wound-Bed Surface}

\textbf{Camera model.} Every camera is a \emph{pinhole} camera. A 3D point
$X$ (in world coordinates) is first moved into the camera's own coordinates,
$X_c = RX + t$, where the rotation $R$ and shift $t$ describe the camera's
pose. It then lands on pixel $(u,v)$ with
$u = f\,X_{c,x}/X_{c,z} + c_x$ and $v = f\,X_{c,y}/X_{c,z} + c_y$. Here
$f$ is the focal length in pixels and $(c_x,c_y)$ is the image center.
$X_{c,z}$ is the \emph{depth}: how far the point is in front of the camera.
Running this backwards gives a 3D point from a pixel and its depth:
$X = R^{\top}(Z\,K^{-1}[u,v,1]^{\top} - t)$, where $K$ holds $f$, $c_x$, and
$c_y$. Every route below uses these two formulas.

\textbf{RGB-only route.} Here, the software has only color images.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It estimates every camera pose from the images themselves with
COLMAP structure from motion (SfM). It does not use the dataset's poses,
because a real color camera would not come with them. SfM finds the same
features in several images and solves for the camera positions that explain
them.
\item It pairs each view with the next four views in the sequence and
rectifies each pair with OpenCV. Rectification rotates the two images so
that a point in one image appears on the same row in the other. The search
for matches then becomes one-dimensional.
\item For each pair, OpenCV's semi-global block matcher (\texttt{StereoSGBM},
3-way mode) finds the horizontal shift, or \emph{disparity}, of every pixel.
Nearer points shift more. Settings: block size 5, smoothness penalties
$P_1=200$ and $P_2=800$, uniqueness ratio 10, speckle window 80 with range
2, and left--right difference 1. The maximum disparity is 128 pixels in the
sensor study and 192 in the SALVE and synthetic-control runs, rounded down
to a multiple of 16 and capped at one third of the image width. The matcher
runs in both directions. A pixel is kept only if the two answers agree
within 1.5 pixels. This removes most false matches.
\item Each kept pixel becomes a 3D point. Only points that project inside
the reference view's wound mask, dilated by a $51\times51$ pixel square, are
kept. The dilation keeps a thin strip of skin around the wound so that the
edge is not cut off.
\item The points are flattened onto a 2D parameter plane and connected into
triangles by Delaunay triangulation. A triangle is dropped if any of its
edges is longer than four times the median distance between neighboring
points. This stops the mesh from bridging holes where there is no data.
\item Because SfM cannot know true size, the result has an unknown scale.
\emph{For evaluation only}, the estimated camera centers are matched to the
dataset's camera centers with a similarity transform: one rotation, one
translation, and one scale, solved in closed form with a singular value
decomposition (the Umeyama method). Because this uses ground truth, it does
not prove that the system can measure size on its own.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Depth-assisted (RGB-D) routes.} In these routes, depth is
\emph{rendered}, not measured. Open3D's ray-casting scene shoots one ray
through every pixel of a dataset camera and records where the ray first
hits the dataset mesh. That distance along the camera's viewing axis is the
depth. Each depth pixel is turned into a 3D point with the formula above.
The points from all selected views are combined. A pixel is kept only when
at least three view pairs support it, and its value is the median of those
pairs. The median ignores single bad values. Triangles are then built as in
the RGB-only route.

Three depth routes are compared: \texttt{RGBD\_ORACLE} (clean rendered
depth), \texttt{RGBD\_D405\_LIKE} (rendered depth with the noise model
below), and \texttt{HIGH\_RES\_RGB\_PLUS\_DEPTH}. The last one uses the same
depth but takes its wound mask from the full-resolution color image after
downsampling and nearest-neighbor upsampling. It tests whether a sharper
color mask helps; it is not a second physical camera.

\textbf{Which views.} RGB routes are run with 10, 20, 30, or all views of a
sequence. Depth routes use 20 views. The views are split into two disjoint
groups, A and B, by alternating them. Each group is then sampled evenly
along the sequence. Running A and B separately shows whether the result
repeats when the cameras change. Because both groups see the same wound,
they are not independent samples.

\subsection{Step 2: Stress-Testing the Depth Input}

To imitate problems a real sensor could have, the rendered depth is damaged
on purpose:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Missing depth:} each pixel is deleted independently with
probability 10, 30, 50, 70, 85, or 95\%.
\item \emph{Noise:} Gaussian noise with standard deviation
$\sigma = 0.005\,W\,(Z/\tilde Z)^2$, where $W$ is wound width, $Z$ is the
pixel's depth, and $\tilde Z$ is the median depth. Real depth cameras get
noisier with distance, roughly with its square, which is why this form was
used. A high-noise profile uses $\sigma = 0.05\,W$. Values are also rounded
to steps of $0.001\,W$ to mimic a sensor's limited resolution.
\item \emph{Misalignment:} the depth image is shifted by 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, or 20
pixels, rotated by $\pm0.25$, $\pm0.5$, or $\pm1$ degrees, or scaled by
0.95--1.05. In each case, color and depth no longer describe the same
spot.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 3: Scoring a Reconstructed Surface}

The reference is the dataset's own mesh, cut to the region marked by the
frozen color mask. It is the surface that the images were rendered from,
not an independent medical measurement.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Wound width} $W$: the mask's horizontal extent in pixels,
converted to dataset units as median depth $\times$ pixels $/ f$.
\item \emph{MAE} and \emph{P95}: for every reconstructed point, find the
distance to the nearest point on the reference mesh. Report the mean
and the 95th percentile, divided by $W$.
\item \emph{Completeness}: the share of reference points that have a
reconstructed point within $0.05\,W$. We also report 1\% and 2\% of $W$.
\item \emph{Reference-volume error}: $|V_\text{pred}-V_\text{ref}|/V_\text{ref}$.
Each $V$ adds up the depth below a fitted skin surface over the supported
triangles. It depends on the chosen skin surface, so it is not the true
amount of missing tissue.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 4: Deciding Whether a Route Is Good Enough}

A route passes the \emph{surface gate} if all four of these hold: at least
70\% of points are stable between groups A and B; completeness is at least
80\%; MAE is at most 5\% of $W$; and at least 80\% of points survive the
filters. The \emph{measurement gate} also requires perimeter error at most
20\%, maximum-depth error at most 5\% of $W$, and reference-volume error at
most 20\%. The router then returns one label: \texttt{ACCEPT},
\texttt{INSUFFICIENT\_GEOMETRY}, \texttt{INSUFFICIENT\_SCALE},
\texttt{FALLBACK\_TO\_RGB}, \texttt{FALLBACK\_TO\_RGBD},
\texttt{RESCAN}, \texttt{RGB\_DEPTH\_CALIBRATION\_FAILED}, or
\texttt{REFUSE\_TO\_PRINT}. It always sets \texttt{print\_authorized} to
false. Groups A and B are scored separately, and their agreement is
reported. These thresholds are engineering choices for this study; they
are not clinical standards.

\subsection{Step 5: Checking the Camera Calibration}

If color and depth do not line up, a measurement can look repeatable and
still be wrong (Fig.~\ref{fig:misalignment}). We therefore built a virtual
calibration target and tried to detect injected faults.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Target:} a flat plate with a $10\times7$ checkerboard (30~mm
squares, 21~mm markers, $0.30\times0.21$~m board) and three
$45\times45$~mm blocks of height 20, 40, and 60~mm. It is rendered at
$640\times480$ pixels from 1.0~m. The steps let the software check depth
in three dimensions, not just on a flat plane.
\item \emph{Faults:} translation offsets of $\pm0.0025$, $\pm0.01$, and
$\pm0.05$ (in the notebook's fractional units); rotations ($\pm0.1$ to $\pm5$ degrees); depth-scale errors
($\pm0.5$ to $\pm10\%$); constant, linear, and nonlinear depth bias; focal
length errors ($\pm1$, 3, or 5\%); image-center shifts; lens distortion;
one-frame timing errors; and combinations.
\item \emph{Detector:} on each configuration it computes reprojection
error, depth scale, rigid-fit error, color-to-depth rotation and shift,
plane fit, step-height error, normal angle, and agreement between views.
Each check has a limit equal to the larger of $1.5\times$ the worst value
seen on clean training data and a fixed floor (for example 0.5 pixels for
reprojection or 0.15 degrees for angles). A configuration is flagged if any
check exceeds its limit.
\item \emph{Test set:} 48 clean, 168 faulty, and 24 target-design stress
configurations, plus 480 diagnostic wound subsets from the same four wound
sequences. The limits were frozen on separate training configurations
before this set was scored.
\item \emph{What counts as dangerous:} a fault is dangerous if it changes
the reference wound volume or depth by more than 5, 10, or 20\%. This is
judged by its effect, not by its size. \emph{Recall} is the number of
dangerous configurations flagged divided by all dangerous configurations.
The \emph{false-alarm rate} is the number of clean configurations flagged
divided by all clean configurations.
\item \emph{Pass rule:} at least 95\% recall above 10\% volume error and
100\% recall above 20\% volume error, plus no accepted case with
surface-support failure, at most 10\% false alarms, and at least 40 clean
controls.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 6: Can a Clean Measurement Be Trusted?}

As a further check, we took 24 clean, non-oracle variants from the four
sequences and asked the software to accept or reject each measurement.
Acceptance required: support at least 95\%; integration residual at most
1\%; volume uncertainty at most 10\%; depth uncertainty at most 20\%;
volume near the boundary at most 10\%; volume that depends on the reference
surface at most 10\%; and depth signal at least five times its
uncertainty.

\subsection{Step 7: Can Hidden Healthy Skin Be Predicted?}

This test asks whether healthy skin around a hole is enough to predict the
skin inside it. On the held-out body model \texttt{subject\_2}, the software
cuts a circular \emph{disc} from intact skin (for example, a shoulder
disc with diameter 0.0153~DU). It keeps a visible \emph{ring} around the
disc that is 0.2 disc diameters wide. A thin-plate spline (TPS) is fitted to
the ring heights. A TPS is the smoothest surface that passes near the given
points, much like a thin metal sheet bent through them. The TPS then
predicts the hidden disc. An uncertainty envelope (lower and upper height)
comes from a calibrated ensemble, aiming for 90\% coverage. The method and
envelope were frozen before the hidden heights were used. There were 55
discs, giving 330 disc--ring records, 297 of them valid. Error is reported
as a share of disc diameter. Coverage is the share of hidden points whose
true height lies inside the envelope.

\emph{Exact settings.} Ring widths were 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, and
0.40 disc diameters. Two patches were drawn per hole specification. A ring
was usable only if it had at least 30 skin samples covering at least 9 of 12
angular sectors. Predictions were made on a $31\times31$ grid spanning
$\pm0.65$ diameters around the disc center. The TPS is SciPy's
\texttt{RBFInterpolator} with \texttt{kernel="thin\_plate\_spline"} and
\texttt{smoothing=1e-7}, fitted to at most 350 evenly spaced ring points.
Six surface models were compared on a separate SELECTION split
(\texttt{subject\_1} shoulder patches): local plane, RANSAC plane,
quadratic, TPS, cubic B-spline, and local RBF. TPS had the lowest mean
absolute error, 0.000343 disc diameters (B-spline 0.000401, quadratic
0.000422), so it was frozen before the TEST split was opened. The
envelope uses an ensemble of three models (TPS, B-spline, quadratic). At
each point, let $m$ be their median and $h=(\max-\min)/2$ their half-spread.
The envelope is $m \pm k\,h$, where $k=2.0924$ is the 90th percentile of
$|\text{error}|/h$ over the 19{,}225 SELECTION points (with $k\ge1$). The
B-spline is a cubic tensor-product spline in normalized coordinates. Its
knots are $-1.2$ (repeated four times), $-0.6$, $0$, $0.6$, and $1.2$
(repeated four times). It uses a ridge penalty of $10^{-5}$ and is fitted to
what is left after
removing a best-fit plane. These values are recorded in
\texttt{frozen\_decisions.json} in the folder
\texttt{results/\allowbreak rgb\_wound/\allowbreak healthy\_skin\_hidden\_disc\_v3/}.
The generating script itself is not part of the repository.

\subsection{Step 8: Direct Target or Staged Filling?}

Each of the 18 cases starts from known healthy skin on \texttt{subject\_2}
(an arm and a shoulder patch). The software carves a shallow, medium, or
deep cavity. It then leaves the edge undeformed or applies moderate or
strong deformation. Three depths, two regions, and three edge conditions
make 18 cases. One \emph{declared layer} is 1\% of the opening diameter.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Direct:} predict the original surface from the ring with the
frozen TPS, then fill to it.
\item \emph{Staged:} using the lower envelope as the ceiling, add one layer
where there is room, re-measure, and repeat. Stop when less than one layer
of supported room is left, or when no safe region remains.
\end{itemize}
The hidden original skin is used only afterward to score overfill and
underfill. Direct filling was to be chosen only if coverage was at least
90\%, maximum overfill stayed below one layer, no boundary or unsupported
deposits occurred, and moderate deformation was handled acceptably.

\emph{Exact cavity and edge shapes.} The two source patches are
\texttt{subject\_2\_arm\_8e41ae1b\_percent\_20\_circle\_00} and
\texttt{subject\_2\_shoulder\_d16cd16a\_percent\_20\_circle\_00}, each with
a 0.40-diameter ring. Let $D$ be the opening diameter and $r$ the distance
from its center in diameters. The cavity depth below the healthy skin is
$d(r) = p\,D\,(1-(r/0.5)^2)^{1.5}$ for $r\le0.5$, with peak $p=0.02$,
0.05, or 0.10 (shallow, medium, deep). This shape is deepest at the center
and meets the skin smoothly at the edge. The edge deformation adds
$A\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12[(r-0.5)D/(0.075D)]^2\big)\cos(2\theta+0.37)$, a
narrow wave around the rim, with $A$ equal to 0, 0.5, or 1.5 layers. The
planner's ceiling is the lower edge of the frozen ensemble envelope from
Step~7.

\subsection{Step 9: The Five-Case Offline Demonstration}

\texttt{robot/simulation/observable\_demo.py} builds a synthetic scene. It
contains a round wound in a colored skin image and a bowl-shaped
(parabolic) cavity in a depth image. It then applies each case's
damage: color only, lower resolution, Gaussian noise, missing pixels, or a
horizontal shift between color and depth. The router requires all of the
following:
\begin{itemize}
\item the wound boundary is at least 100 pixels wide;
\item depth coverage is at least 70\%;
\item depth noise is at most 2\% of the scene diameter;
\item color--depth disagreement is at most 10\%;
\item if reference estimates disagree by more than 0.01 scene units, an
external target is given.
\end{itemize}
These limits were set so that each case A--E tests one condition.

A planned deposit must lie within 90\% of the wound radius, outside a
no-go zone, and where uncertainty is at most half a layer. Each stage adds
at most one layer (0.01 scene units), and the nozzle is held 0.02 units
above the surface along its normal. A simulated $24\times64$ surface is then
updated, for at most seven stages.

\subsection{Step 10: The Unified and Patient-Level Pipelines}

The unified pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:unified}) chains the same parts on
the five synthetic cases. The patient-level pipeline does the same on a
rendered human leg, so that the output is a scaffold sitting on a body. We
describe it in full because it is the end point of the whole method
(Fig.~\ref{fig:patient}; parameters in Table~\ref{tab:patientparams}).

\textbf{Data.} We use Syn3DWound sequence
\texttt{subject\_1/Syn3DWound\_0415\_leg\_strong\_lighting\_phi}. It
provides 40 rendered photographs at $4000\times2160$ pixels, a wound mask for
each photograph, COLMAP camera files ($f=2222.2$ pixels,
$c_x=2000$, $c_y=1080$), and the leg mesh (11{,}087 points, 21{,}692
triangles). The mesh file stores ``up'' as its second axis while the cameras
use the third, so every mesh point $(x,y,z)$ is rewritten as $(x,-z,y)$.
This change puts the mesh and the cameras in the same coordinates.

\textbf{(a) Finding the wound on the 3D leg.} The masks are 2D. To label 3D
points, we project every mesh point into every photograph. A view gives a
\emph{vote} only if the point faces the camera, meaning its surface normal
points toward the camera center. This stops points on the far side of the
leg from voting. The vote is ``wound'' if the mask pixel is brighter than
40 out of 255. The masks are shaded, not pure black and white: the wound is
above 40, while the skin and background are below about 60 and mostly below
10. A point is labeled wound if more than half of the views that see it say
so. A majority is used because a single view can be wrong at the wound edge.
Finally, we keep only the largest connected group of wound points, which
removes isolated mistakes. Result: 153 wound points, each seen by at least
39 views.

\textbf{(b) A local coordinate frame.} The leg is curved, but near the wound
it is almost flat. We take all mesh points within 2.5 wound radii of the
wound center and find their best-fitting plane by principal component
analysis (PCA). The direction of least spread is the skin normal $n$, flipped
if needed to point toward the cameras. The first in-plane axis $e_1$ is the
wound's longest direction, found by PCA on the wound points projected into
the plane; $e_2 = n \times e_1$. Lining $e_1$ up with the wound means that
the grid in the next step follows the wound's shape.

\textbf{(c) A height map.} In this frame, every mesh point has in-plane
coordinates $(x,y)$ and a height $z$ along $n$. We sample $z$ on a square
grid with 0.1~mDU spacing that extends $\pm1.5$ wound radii, using linear
interpolation between mesh triangles (\texttt{scipy.griddata}). The same
interpolation of the 0/1 wound labels, thresholded at 0.5, gives the wound
region on the grid. A grid is used because the filling rules are easiest to
apply pixel by pixel.

\textbf{(d) The target surface.} The \emph{healthy ring} is every grid
pixel outside the wound and within 0.4 wound radii of its edge. We fit a
quadratic surface
$z = a_0 + a_1x + a_2y + a_3x^2 + a_4xy + a_5y^2$ to the ring by least
squares. A quadratic can follow a gently curved limb but cannot invent
small bumps. The root-mean-square misfit on the ring was 0.164~mDU. This
surface plays the role of the \emph{operator's target}. Step~8 showed that
the true pre-wound skin cannot be recovered, so the target is a stand-in
that an operator would have to accept, not anatomy.

\textbf{(e) Where filling is allowed.} A grid pixel is \emph{supported} if
it has a height and lies inside the wound. Its distance from the wound edge
must be at least 10\% of half the wound's long width. Its measurement
uncertainty $u$ must be at most half a layer. We use one layer
$=0.2$~mDU and $u=0.02$~mDU. The margin keeps the nozzle away from the
fragile edge. The uncertainty rule means that the software does not print
where it is unsure of the surface height.

\textbf{(f) Sorting every pixel into one category.} Before placing any
cell, the software labels every grid pixel with exactly one category, in
this order: \emph{outside the wound}; \emph{boundary margin} (inside the
wound but within the edge margin); \emph{no support} (no measured height);
\emph{uncertainty gate} ($u$ above half a layer); \emph{below one layer}
(less than one layer of room under the lower uncertainty envelope, defined
in (h)); and \emph{eligible}. Only eligible pixels may ever receive
material. After planning, eligible pixels inside an accepted cell are
relabeled \emph{covered}; the rest are \emph{eligible but uncovered}. These
are the colors of the coverage map (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}). The rules are
exactly those of (e); the categories only make visible where each rule
bites.

\textbf{(g) Choosing the honeycomb cells.} A honeycomb is a grid of
hexagons. Flat-topped hexagons with side $s$ sit on a lattice whose two
step vectors are $s(1.5,\sqrt3/2)$ and $s(0,\sqrt3)$. Each grid pixel
belongs to its nearest hexagon center, which divides the plane into
hexagons. A lattice has three free settings: the side $s$, a rotation
$\theta$, and a shift (offset) of the whole lattice.

\emph{The first planner and why it failed.} Our first version reused the
generator of the project's cylindrical planner (\texttt{modules/honeycomb.py}).
It fixed $\theta=0$ and no shift, and set $s$ so that six hexagons span the
wound's narrow width ($s=0.78$~mDU). A cell was kept only if its
\emph{whole} hexagon was supported. Only eight cells passed, all in one
strip along the middle of the wound. They covered 42.4\% of the eligible
area and 22.9\% of the wound. All 42 rejected cells that touched the wound
were rejected for the same reason: the complete hexagon did not fit inside
the supported region. None was rejected for uncertainty or missing support.
The safety rules were therefore not the problem. Large, fixed hexagons simply
do not fit a thin, curved region.

\emph{The search.} We kept every safety rule and searched the lattice
instead. The side $s$ takes five evenly spaced values from 0.78~mDU down to
0.40~mDU. The smallest side is two strand widths. We assume a printed strand
is as wide as one layer is thick (0.2~mDU), and a hexagon with a shorter side
would let its opposite walls merge and close the pore. The rotation $\theta$
takes six values, 0, 10, \ldots, 50 degrees; a hexagonal lattice looks the
same after 60 degrees, so this covers all distinct angles. The shift takes 16
values: $(i/4)$ and $(j/4)$ of the two step vectors for
$i,j\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. This gives $5\times6\times16=480$ lattices. The lattice
is anchored at the center of the eligible region. Three planners were
built from this search and evaluated separately:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{WHOLE\_CELL}: from each lattice keep the hexagons whose every
pixel, and every point of whose nozzle path, is eligible. Choose the lattice
with the most covered eligible pixels.
\item \texttt{ADAPTIVE\_WHOLE\_CELL}: take the best \texttt{WHOLE\_CELL}
lattice for each side. Then, from the next smaller side downward, add whole
hexagons that fit in the still-uncovered eligible area, as long as their
paths do not cross existing paths. Smaller cells near the edge follow the
curved boundary better.
\item \texttt{CLIPPED\_BOUNDARY}: keep the accepted whole hexagons, and also
cut each partly eligible hexagon down to its eligible part, the way a 3D
printer's slicer cuts a pattern to fit inside the part's outline. A cut
piece is kept only if it is printable. It must survive an
\emph{opening} with a disk two strands wide (about 5 pixels). An opening
erases any part too narrow for that disk to fit inside. The surviving piece
must cover at least a quarter of a full hexagon, and it must stay one
connected piece after shrinking by one pixel. Its nozzle path is the outline
of that shrunk piece, so the path lies strictly inside eligible pixels.
\end{itemize}
Every planner then goes through the same staged fill (h). The pass gate is
fixed in advance. The software reports \emph{eligible coverage}, the share of
eligible pixels inside cells that received at least one layer, and also
\emph{total wound coverage}, the same footprint divided by the whole wound
area. A planner is \texttt{REJECTED} if it has any violation or fills no
cell. It is \texttt{COMPLETE} if eligible coverage is at least 90\% with zero
violations, and \texttt{PARTIAL} otherwise. The violations counted are:
deposits or path points outside the wound, in the margin, or on
unsupported pixels; deposits above the lower envelope; deposits outside the
eligible region; overlapping cells; and crossings between cell paths.
The software picks the first \texttt{COMPLETE} planner in the order
\texttt{WHOLE\_CELL}, \texttt{ADAPTIVE\_WHOLE\_CELL},
\texttt{CLIPPED\_BOUNDARY}, so that regular whole hexagons are preferred and
clipping is used only when needed. If several lattices tie, the larger side
wins because it gives fewer, more regular pores. If no planner passes, the
one with the highest coverage is reported, together with the constraint that
limits it.

\textbf{(h) Filling in stages.} The chosen cells stay fixed for all stages,
so that the walls of each stage stack on the previous one. Each stage first
computes the room left at every supported pixel:
$\text{room} = \text{target} - (\text{measured} + u)$. The value
$\text{target}-u$ is the \emph{lower uncertainty envelope}: adding $u$ means
that we plan against the \emph{highest} plausible current surface, so we
underfill rather than overfill. A cell receives one whole layer only if its
\emph{smallest} room is at least one layer. Otherwise it is skipped in that
stage. This keeps every deposit below the target even at the cell's
shallowest pixel. The price is that a cell spanning deep and shallow parts
stops as soon as its shallowest pixel is full. The simulated surface is
raised by the deposit, then ``re-measured'' by adding Gaussian noise with
$\sigma=u/3$ (seed 0). The next stage plans only from this new measurement.
Two stop rules: \texttt{SUPPORTED\_DEPTH\_BELOW\_ONE\_LAYER} if the largest
supported room is under one layer, and \texttt{NO\_SAFE\_SUPPORTED\_REGION}
if no cell qualifies. At most ten stages are allowed. For simplicity, a
layer is added over the cell's whole footprint even though the path traces
only its outline. This simplification is listed in the limitations.

\textbf{(i) The nozzle path.} For each active whole hexagon, the path
traces the hexagon's outline, shrunk to 90\% so that neighboring walls do
not overlap, with four points per edge, and then moves to the center. A
clipped cell's path is its closed inner outline (see (g)). Heights come from
the planned top surface. The nozzle direction is the normal of a plane
fitted by least squares to the cell's current surface. One plane per cell
keeps the nozzle steady within a cell instead of wobbling with every pixel.
Every point is held 0.4~mDU above the surface along that direction.
Between cells, the nozzle lifts by 0.8~mDU so that it does not drag
material. The visiting order starts with the cell having the smallest
grid row and always moves to the nearest unvisited cell. It is then
improved by \emph{2-opt}: any two links in the route are swapped whenever
reversing the part between them shortens it, until no swap helps. The
improved route is kept only if it is shorter. Finally, every point is moved
back to leg coordinates
($\text{world} = \text{origin} + x\,e_1 + y\,e_2 + z\,n$). A rotation matrix
whose tool axis points into the surface is attached with the original
project's \texttt{compute\_nozzle\_orientations}.

\textbf{(j) Showing it on the patient.} The view whose direction is closest
to the skin normal (\texttt{image\_000}) is chosen for display. The filled
cells are drawn as prisms on the leg mesh. Their heights are
multiplied by four only in the 3D panel, so that thin layers are visible.
The wound outline, the eligible-region outline, the accepted cell
footprints, the uncovered eligible pixels, and the toolpath are projected
into the photograph with the camera formula from Step~1. A status box
prints the two coverage numbers and the planner status. Every input file's
SHA-256 fingerprint is saved in \texttt{provenance.json}, so others can
check that they use the same data.

\subsection{How Many Independent Samples Are There?}

The sensor-route study contains 252 route/subset results across four wound
sequence contexts. A subset is a selected group of views from a sequence,
not a new wound. The six routes in Fig.~\ref{fig:routes} are a focused part
of that study. The four sequences are Syn3DWound
\texttt{subject\_1/\ldots0023\_shoulder\_\allowbreak weak\_lighting\_theta},
\texttt{subject\_1/\ldots0022\_sacrum\_\allowbreak weak\_lighting\_phi},
\texttt{subject\_2/\ldots100487\_arm\_\allowbreak weak\_lighting\_theta}, and
\texttt{subject\_2/\ldots case24\_day1\_scene1\_raw\_hand\_\allowbreak strong\_lighting\_theta}
(full names in the notebook output). The sacrum sequence shows the same
source wound as an earlier baseline, so it is not an independent wound. The
calibration test reuses the same four sequences. The healthy-disc test and the 18-case
comparison each use one held-out body model. The patient-level run uses one
rendered leg. Repeated views, pixels, subsets, and synthetic configurations
share underlying data and are not independent patients. We therefore report
descriptive results rather than population-level confidence intervals.
The metric audit records the available denominators, units, and origins.

\subsection{How the Input Route Changes the Result}

Figure~\ref{fig:routes} compares six routes. Its left panel reports the mean
distance error of the visible surface; its right panel reports error in a
reference-defined volume. Both use percentages, but their denominators are
different: wound width on the left and a computed reference volume on the
right. A small bar on one side does not automatically imply a small bar on
the other.

The aligned RGB-D route had a mean surface error of 0.36\% of wound width
and a mean reference-volume error of 4.55\%. The misaligned depth route had
1.98\% and 44.11\%, respectively; its worst available reference-volume
error was 80.55\%. These comparisons use synthetic depth and a synthetic
reference, so they do not establish how a real camera would perform.

Table~\ref{tab:routes} also distinguishes evaluation checks from the
observable router. To pass the surface check, a subset needed stable
observations over at least 70\% of the wound mask, at least 80\% completeness
within 5\% of wound width, and a mean surface error no greater than 5\% of
width. At least 80\% of its mesh triangles also had to belong to the largest
connected piece. The measurement check additionally required no more than
20\% error in mesh boundary length, no more than 5\% of wound width in
maximum reference-dependent depth error, and no more than 20\% error in
reference-defined volume. Missing measurements did not pass. These are
experimental thresholds, not medical criteria.

The two checks use synthetic reference geometry to evaluate the result. The
router instead uses information available to the system, such as depth
coverage, boundary agreement, image resolution, and consistency between
views. It can therefore disagree with the reference-based checks. For each
route, the counts in the table are over four sequence contexts: a context
counts as a pass only when all its route subsets satisfy the corresponding
check. The high-resolution RGB plus depth route passed the surface and
measurement checks in all four contexts, but the router accepted only one.
Conversely, the router rejected all four contexts for each of the
low-coverage, high-noise, and misaligned routes. These counts describe the
tested configurations, not a guarantee of detecting every future fault.

\subsection{Can Calibration Checks Detect a Problem?}

Calibration describes how image and depth observations are related.
If that relationship is wrong, a color boundary may be placed over the
wrong part of a depth map. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} examines whether
the frozen checks detect such synthetic problems. The left graph uses
\emph{recall}: the fraction of configurations with a specified consequence
that were flagged. The consequences are defined using a computed
reference-volume error, not a clinical outcome.

At the 5\% threshold, the detector flagged 73 of 112 configurations with
total reference-volume error above that threshold. It also flagged 41 of
42 configurations meeting the separate 5\% incremental-error criterion,
which compares the faulty result with a matched clean reconstruction.
These are different groups and different error definitions; the two
fractions should not be read as a direct head-to-head comparison on the same
cases. Among 48 clean configurations, two were flagged. Five diagnostic
subsets were unavailable in the threshold-recall evaluation and were not
silently treated as zero error. The overall engineering gate failed despite
the favorable incremental-error fractions. Table~\ref{tab:calibration}
gives the exact counts at all three thresholds.

The right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration} shows a separate target-design
comparison. A calibration target is a known shape observed to help check
the relationship between color and depth. Here, it is simulated rather than
a physical object. A stepped target has changes in height, while a planar
target is flat. The stepped target rejected 65 of 72 fault configurations,
compared with 41 of 72 for the planar target; each had zero false alarms
among 24 clean controls in that separate test. These are synthetic target
checks, not proof that either target will work with a real device.

\subsection{Why the Original Healthy Surface Remains Unknown}

Suppose a circular part of an image is covered by a sticker. Even if the
area around the sticker is visible, more than one smooth surface can connect
its edges. The surrounding ring alone does not reveal which surface was
actually hidden. The same issue arises when a wound removes or changes the
surface that existed before injury.

Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity} compares six mathematical rules for extending
healthy-ring geometry across a wound. They yield different reference
surfaces and therefore different computed volumes. The rules are candidate
models, not six verified versions of the original skin. The separate
clean-measurement validity test accepted 0 of 24 evaluated variants. Its
dominant limitation was the unknown healthy-skin closure and instability of
extrapolation from the visible ring.

The healthy-disc test in Fig.~\ref{fig:hidden} makes a limited version of
this question measurable: it hides intact skin for which the original
surface is available to the evaluator. Across 330 patch-and-ring records,
297 had valid TPS predictions. The average of the valid records' mean

uncertainty envelope covered 88.32\% of evaluated points, below its 90\%
target.

Those small errors apply to an easier intact-skin test. The test does not
include the full effects of injury, such as swelling, changed edges, or
missing tissue, and its held-out patches come from only one body model.
Therefore, it is a simplified test of extrapolation, not proof that
pre-wound anatomy can be recovered.

\subsection{Architecture Decision and Offline Demonstration}

The 18-case comparison starts with known synthetic healthy surfaces, carves
wounds into them, and then hides the original surfaces from the simulated
online policy. The cases combine an arm and a shoulder region, shallow,
medium, and deep cavities, and no, moderate, or strong edge deformation.
Here, one declared layer is 1\% of the opening diameter in synthetic units;
it is not a physical layer thickness in millimeters.

The direct option uses the surrounding ring to predict a final reference
surface. The staged option uses the lower edge of the frozen uncertainty
envelope to identify supported regions for partial filling in the
simulation. The software updates the simulated geometry after a stage and
stops when there is no supported region with at least one layer of
remaining depth to that lower bound. The hidden original surface is used
only afterward to score overfill and underfill. The experiment does not
validate an operator's final target.

Direct reference alone was to be selected only if all predefined checks
passed: at least 90\% uncertainty coverage, maximum overfill below one
declared layer, no boundary or unsupported-deposition violations, and
acceptable performance under moderate edge deformation. The direct option
failed this decision gate. The selected design is therefore staged partial
filling with an operator-defined final target.

This decision does \emph{not} establish that staged filling is safe. In the
strong-deformation cases, the reported maximum staged-policy overfill
relative to the hidden reference reached 1.18 declared layers, and
boundary-height violations were recorded. Some of these measurements can
reflect the deformed starting geometry, so they should not all be
interpreted as newly deposited material. They show why an operator-defined
target and further physical validation cannot be replaced by this
simulation.

The separate five-case demonstration tests how software decisions are
reported. In case A, the software produced a three-stage simulation plan.
In cases B--E, it returned a need to stop or obtain more information because
of boundary resolution, missing depth, misalignment, or an unspecified
target. All five decisions matched their expected software outcomes, with
zero recorded boundary or exclusion-zone violations in this demonstration
(Table~\ref{tab:cases}). This result must not be confused with the
violations in the separate 18-case comparison. Figure~\ref{fig:staged}
illustrates the three-stage case; no material was deposited.

\subsection{Putting the Pieces Together: One Pipeline}\label{sec:unified}

The earlier subsections test the parts one at a time. A practical system
must run them in sequence, using each part's output as the next part's input.
The repository therefore includes one program that runs seven stages in a
fixed order (Figure~\ref{fig:unified}). It uses the same five synthetic
cases as Table~\ref{tab:cases}. Any stage can stop the run and report its
reason. That stop counts as a valid result, not a crash.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Observe.} A synthetic camera records a color image and, when
available, a depth image (panel a).
\item \textbf{Identify the wound.} The program marks which pixels belong to
the wound. In this synthetic image, that region has a distinct color. On real
images, this mask would come from the reconstruction notebook. The
identified region overlapped the renderer's own wound region by 95.7\%,
where overlap is shared pixels divided by all pixels marked by either one.
\item \textbf{Measure the surface.} Inside the wound mask, each depth value
becomes a height relative to the surrounding skin plane. Negative numbers
are below the skin (panel c).
\item \textbf{Route.} The same quality checks used above test boundary
resolution, depth coverage, noise, and color--depth alignment. Cases B--E
stop here, for the same reasons as in Table~\ref{tab:cases}.
\item \textbf{Require a target.} The previous subsections showed that the
original healthy surface cannot be recovered from the images. Therefore, the
program continues only if an operator supplies the final height. In case A,
this is a synthetic test plane at the skin level. When the same case is run
without an operator target, the program stops with
\texttt{OPERATOR\_FINAL\_TARGET\_REQUIRED}.
\item \textbf{Lay out the honeycomb.} The hexagonal grid from the project's
original honeycomb planner is placed over the identified wound. That planner
originally wrapped the grid around a known cylinder. Here, each hexagon is
instead placed on the \emph{measured} wound bed. A cell is used only if
every pixel inside it has valid depth, lies inside the safety margin from
the wound edge, and has low enough measurement uncertainty. In case A, 23
cells with a side length of 8.5~mm at the synthetic scale passed this test
(panel b).
\item \textbf{Fill in stages.} In each stage, the program plans at most one
layer of new height inside the accepted cells. That height never goes above
the lower edge of the uncertainty range. The nozzle path traces each cell's
walls at the new top surface. It is tilted to match the slope of the surface
inside that cell, and it lifts between cells (panel d). After each stage,
the simulated surface is measured again, and the next plan uses this new
measurement.
\end{enumerate}

In case A, the program planned three stages: 23, 23, and 19 active cells
(panel e). It then stopped with \texttt{NO\_SAFE\_SUPPORTED\_REGION}. The
center had been filled to within one layer of the target, but the remaining
depth lay in a rim near the wound edge, where no whole cell fits inside the
safety margin. The program reports this rim; it does not guess how to fill
it. In this run, the accepted cells covered 55.2\% of the wound area. At the
end, 13.0\% of the wound area remained at least one layer below the target.
The program recorded zero boundary violations and zero overfill. Overfill
was scored only afterward against the hidden synthetic truth, which the
planner never used.

This run shows that the parts fit together and that the stop rules work as
designed. It does not show that the measurements are accurate on real
wounds, that a robot can follow the path, or that material would behave
like the simulated layers.

\subsection{The Full Pipeline on a Patient Leg}

Figure~\ref{fig:patient} shows the complete chain on one rendered human leg.
It goes from the photographs to a staged honeycomb scaffold sitting on the
wound. The method is Step~10 in the Methods section, and every number below
is read from the run's \texttt{summary.json} and \texttt{coverage\_audit.json}.

The vote over 40 views marked 153 mesh points as wound (stage 2 panels). The
wound is long and narrow: 15.4~mDU along its main axis and 4.7~mDU across,
with an area of 54.1~mDU$^2$. The quadratic target fits the healthy ring
with a root-mean-square misfit of 0.164~mDU. Before filling, the median
distance from the wound bed up to the target was 0.46~mDU, and the maximum
was 0.58~mDU, or about two to three layers (panel 3--4).

\emph{Where filling was allowed.} Of the 54.1~mDU$^2$ of wound, 24.9~mDU$^2$
is the edge margin, where the nozzle is not allowed. The remaining
29.2~mDU$^2$ has a measured surface. Of that, 29.17~mDU$^2$ is
\emph{eligible}: it passes every rule and has at least one layer of room.
No pixel failed the uncertainty rule and none lacked a surface. So the edge
margin alone caps total wound coverage at 53.9\%, whatever the planner does.

\emph{The first planner failed on coverage.} The first version placed eight
hexagons in one central strip (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}A). They covered only
42.4\% of the eligible area and 22.9\% of the wound, leaving 16.8~mDU$^2$ of
eligible area uncovered. Fifty lattice cells touched the wound; 42 were
rejected. Every one of the 42 was rejected because its complete hexagon did
not fit inside the safe region (panel E). By first reason, 31 cells reached
outside the wound and 11 entered the edge margin. None was rejected for
uncertainty or missing support. Twenty-five rejected cells contained a total
of 16.8~mDU$^2$ of eligible area. The run filled 8 cells in stage 1 and 7 in
stage 2, then stopped with \texttt{NO\_SAFE\_SUPPORTED\_REGION}: no accepted
cell had a full layer of room at every pixel (the best cell's minimum was
0.187~mDU, just under the 0.2~mDU layer). At that moment 1{,}832 supported
pixels still had at least one layer of room, and 1{,}679 of them lay outside
every accepted cell.
This plan is therefore \texttt{PARTIAL}, not a complete scaffold.

\emph{The corrected planner.} Table~\ref{tab:coverage} compares the four
planners under identical safety rules. The best whole-cell lattice uses the
smallest printable side (0.40~mDU) at $50^\circ$ and covers 72.6\% of the
eligible area with 51 cells. Adding smaller cells at the edge did not help.
The best lattice already uses the smallest allowed side, so no smaller
printable size was left. Panel F shows why whole cells fall short:
whole hexagons that are 1.5 or 1.0 strand widths wide would reach 78.6\% and
85.6\%, but they are below the printable minimum and were not allowed. Even
then they would miss the gate. The binding constraint for whole cells is
therefore the minimum printable cell size together with the curved boundary.
Clipping boundary cells to the eligible region removes that limit. The
selected \texttt{CLIPPED\_BOUNDARY} plan (side 0.69~mDU, $30^\circ$) uses 29
cells, 9 whole and 20 clipped. It covers 94.2\% of the eligible area, above
the 90\% gate, and 50.8\% of the whole wound out of a possible 53.9\%. Only
1.68~mDU$^2$ of eligible area stays uncovered (red in panel D and in the
output panel). Across both stages, the plan had zero deposits outside the
wound, zero in the margin, zero on unsupported pixels, zero above the lower
envelope, zero overlapping cells, and zero path crossings. Nothing rose above
the target. Its status is \texttt{COMPLETE} for the coverage gate.

\emph{What is still not done.} \texttt{COMPLETE} refers to the footprint,
not to the depth. Stage 1 filled 29 cells and stage 2 filled 20. The run then
stopped with \texttt{NO\_SAFE\_SUPPORTED\_REGION}: the best cell's minimum
room was 0.171~mDU, under one layer, while the deepest single pixel still had
0.55~mDU of room. This happens because a cell is filled only when \emph{all}
of its pixels have a full layer of room, so a cell that spans a deep center
and a shallow edge stops when its edge is full. At the end, 7.7~mDU$^2$ of
the eligible area was still at least one layer below the lower envelope
(panel H), and 44\% of the wound area was still at least one layer below the
target. The nozzle route, after 2-opt, is 57.1~mDU long instead of 65.2~mDU.
The toolpath file lists 1{,}057 nozzle positions with their tool
orientations in leg coordinates (Fig.~\ref{fig:patient}, output panel). The
geometry is the dataset's rendered mesh, not a depth-camera scan. The units
are not certified millimeters, and the target is a fitted stand-in, not the
patient's original skin. No material was deposited, and printing remains
disabled.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

The most important distinction is between seeing the current surface and
knowing the surface that existed before injury. The tested methods can
describe parts of visible synthetic geometry, but a chosen reference surface
is still an assumption or an external input. The system should not report
the resulting reference-defined volume as the true amount of missing tissue.

The evidence also has limits. The sensor-route depth was rendered from a
mesh, not captured from a wound by a real depth camera. SALVE comparisons
use scale obtained through alignment with scanner ground truth. The
held-out healthy-disc and carved-wound tests each use only one body model,
and repeated subsets and points are correlated. The calibration and
clean-measurement gates failed. The staged policy also showed violations in
the synthetic architecture comparison; a favorable result in the separate
five-case demonstration does not erase them.

The patient-leg scaffold plan has its own limits. Its \textsc{complete}
status means only that accepted cells cover at least 90\% of the eligible
area with zero safety violations. It does not mean that the wound is filled
to the target. Total wound coverage is 50.8\%, because the edge margin is
never eligible, and 7.7~mDU$^2$ of eligible area was still at least one
layer short when the stop rule fired. Twenty of the 29 cells are clipped,
so their pores are not regular hexagons. Whether such pieces print and
behave like whole cells has not been tested. The simulated deposit raises
the whole footprint of a cell by one layer, while the planned path traces
only its outline. The strand width is an assumption, not a measured bead.
The same coverage search was not applied to the five synthetic cases of the
unified pipeline, whose whole-cell plan is reported unchanged. It is a
single rendered leg, so none of these coverage numbers is a population
estimate.

Under the predefined decision rule, direct filling to an inferred reference
alone is not supported. The chosen computational direction is conservative
staged partial filling with an operator-defined final target. It is a design
decision for further validation, not a clinically validated treatment or
permission to print. No real D405 wound dataset, physical depth-sensor
validation, CoppeliaSim robot execution, clinical validation, or physical
printing was performed. Printing remains disabled. Source-verified
literature review, venue formatting, and author/editorial review remain
before manuscript submission.

\section*{Reproducibility}

Commands, expected inputs, output files, and dataset organization are
documented in \texttt{../docs/reproduction.md} and
\texttt{../docs/dataset\_provenance.md}. The figures and tables are generated
by \texttt{../scripts/build\_publication\_package.py} from the committed
evidence bundle. The notebook's default replay displays saved results
without running the experiments again. Rebuilding Figure~\ref{fig:reconstruction}
also requires the identified public Syn3DWound image; the reproduction guide
explains how to fetch and verify that one file. The unified-pipeline figure
and its toolpath are regenerated with
\texttt{python scripts/run\_unified\_pipeline.py}. The patient-leg figure,
its toolpath, and its input fingerprints are regenerated with
\texttt{python scripts/run\_patient\_pipeline.py}, after downloading the one
Syn3DWound leg sequence described in the dataset guide. The same command
writes the coverage audit figure, \texttt{coverage\_audit.json}, the
480-lattice search table \texttt{coverage\_search.csv}, and the planner
comparison \texttt{coverage\_planners.csv}. Raw datasets and sealed
intermediate result trees are not committed. The source dataset's
noncommercial license and attribution requirements must be considered
before publication.
